# Caesar crosses the Rubicon -> Pharsalus -> Pompey's death (49-48 BC)

## Facts (Wikipedia: "Crossing the Rubicon", "Caesar's civil war", "Battle of Pharsalus", "Pompey"; citing Gruen 1995, Goldsworthy 2006, Beard 2015)
- 7 Jan 49 BC: the Senate demanded Caesar give up his post or be judged an enemy of the state.
- January 49 BC: Caesar crossed the Rubicon with Legio XIII; Suetonius: "alea iacta est" (the die has been cast).
- Pompey, the consuls and much of the Senate left Rome; Pompey escaped from Brundisium east "with almost all of his men and all the ships" (March 49).
- Caesar marched to Spain (Massilia, Ilerda) and defeated the Pompeian army there (49 BC).
- Dyrrhachium (48 BC): Pompey defeated Caesar; Caesar withdrew into Thessaly with supply problems.
- Pharsalus, 9 Aug 48 BC: Caesar 22,000 legionaries + 1,000 cavalry; Pompey 36,000-47,000 legionaries, 5,000-7,000 cavalry.
- Pompey's cavalry pushed back Caesar's cavalry; Caesar revealed a hidden fourth line, whose men leapt up and thrust their pila at the horsemen instead of throwing them; the cavalry panicked and Pompey's army collapsed.
- Pompey fled to Egypt; on arrival (28 Sep 48 BC) he was killed by Lucius Septimius, a former officer of his. Caesar was presented with his head and signet ring and wept (genuineness debated).

## Voice-over
In January, 49 BC, one Roman general crossed a small river, and declared war on Rome itself.
His name was Gaius Julius Caesar. He had just conquered Gaul. Now the Senate sent him an order: give up your army, or be declared an enemy of the state.
He kept the army. With a single legion, he crossed the Rubicon. The die is cast.
Rome panicked. Pompey the Great, the Senate's champion, abandoned the city and sailed to Greece, with almost every ship in Italy.
Caesar couldn't follow. So he turned west, marched to Spain, and destroyed Pompey's legions there first.
Then he came back for Pompey himself. At Dyrrhachium, Pompey beat him. Caesar's hungry army retreated deep into Greece.
Pharsalus, August, 48 BC. Twenty-two thousand men against more than forty thousand. And Pompey had up to seven times more cavalry.
His horsemen swept around Caesar's flank. It should have been over.
Wait. Then how did Caesar win?
You see, Caesar had hidden a fourth line of veterans behind his army. They leapt up and thrust their spears at the riders. The cavalry panicked, and Pompey's whole army collapsed.
Pompey fled to Egypt. The moment he arrived, he was stabbed to death by a man who once served under him. His head was presented to Caesar as a gift. Caesar wept.
So: was Caesar a traitor, or the man who saved Rome from its own Senate?
